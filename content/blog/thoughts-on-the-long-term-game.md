<p>我理解的长期游戏，首先是一个人愿意玩足够久的游戏，理想情况下，是他愿意用一生去玩的游戏。在无数个可选择的游戏中，一个真正的长期游戏应该满足某些条件。那么，这些条件是什么？</p><p>我认为这里说的长期游戏至少是向好、向善、有一定正面效益的。否则，吸毒、赌博也会成为一个毋庸置疑的长期游戏。具体而言，长期游戏应当具有如下的好处：</p><ol><li><p><strong>复利效应</strong>：长期游戏使你能够从复利中获得长期收益。更好的技能会帮助你发展出更好的技能，更深的洞察会帮助你看见更深的东西。你在这个游戏中的投入，包括时间、人、技能、知识和智慧，会变得越来越有效率。</p></li><li><p><strong>独特性</strong>：长期游戏使你能够在某个主题上发展出独特知识，从而让你变得不可替代。如果这种独特性能够和市场需求匹配，市场就可能给予你很大的回报。更进一步，独特性有助于创造责任归属，并进一步带来所有权，比如企业、资产、机会等。</p></li><li><p><strong>存在意义</strong>：长期游戏会给你一种人生意义。它能引导你穿过人生的起伏，占据那些本来可能滋生空虚和虚无的闲暇时间与闲暇心智。</p></li></ol><p>寻找长期游戏的方法似乎仍然很模糊。目前我所知道的，似乎只是“找到你的兴趣”。我们试图通过兴趣找到志向。然而，兴趣很难被抓住，并且会变化。很多时候还会有叶公好龙效应：随着事情逐渐深入，原本有趣的东西反而变得晦涩艰深，让人心生退意。如果足够幸运，或许你能遇到一个一辈子都感兴趣的领域。然而这并不可靠。它看起来更像是在“撞”，完全依靠运气。</p><p>直觉上看，一个长期游戏应该是不功利的。因为人生终有起起落落，一个你愿意在低谷期继续深耕的游戏，不能只是功利的。功利意味着，这个游戏的目标就是带给我钱，让我一直上升。从本质上讲，这种“一直上升”的幻想，和人生必然起伏的现实是冲突的。其次，难道这个游戏帮我赚钱，让我脱离低谷不好吗？一方面，不是所有低谷都与钱有关。认为低谷一定等于没钱，可能是我们这些穷人的幻想。另一方面，我们也应该接受长期游戏中的失败。这个游戏本身应该具有一定意义，能够为人生提供某种支撑。如果期待的是游戏的成功来拯救自己，而不是游戏本身支撑自己，那说明自己仍然处在一个地位游戏中。这直接违反了长期游戏的定义，也就是：你愿意用一辈子玩它。如果这个游戏一旦失败就无法拯救自己，那么你其实一直在玩的只是一个“成功游戏”。而你所选择的那个所谓游戏，也很可能因为无法满足“成功游戏”的要求而被放弃，因此它并不是你真正的长期游戏。</p><p>另一个直觉上成立的条件是，长期游戏应该符合自己的价值观。对于非常清楚自己价值观的人而言，这是一件显而易见的事，他们也更容易找到自己的长期游戏。然而，当然也存在很多价值观并不明确的人。他们同样会对自己应当坚持什么感到迷茫，在变乱的世界中左右摇摆。对于这些人而言，依靠“价值观”去寻找长期游戏，同样并不牢靠。</p><p>但我怀疑，寻找长期游戏是否只有价值观这一条路径？也就是说，你是否必须先明确自己的价值观，才能找到长期游戏？<br/>我觉得长期游戏可以分成两类：</p><ol><li><p><strong>理智上正确的长期游戏</strong>：如果一个游戏从理智上分析，完全符合世界运行规律，符合第一性原理，并且它的结构支持它随着世界的运转而持续存在，那么它就可以成为一个长期游戏。当然，不存在永恒的真理，所以我认为只需要一个比较长期内正确，例如超越个人寿命内是正确的即可。例如，巴菲特的投资理念就是一种结构上让时间站在自己这边的玩法，长期理解并拥有好生意，让资本和判断力在几十年尺度上复利。反例是高杠杆投机（如杰西·利弗莫尔），它很容易让一个人始终暴露在归零风险中，长期参与本身就会不断考验你的脆弱点。即便极度聪明、有判断力、纪律、市场直觉和经验，也无法长期参与。因为知道投机游戏的规则，能够从中赚取巨额财富，不等于这个游戏适合作为长期游戏。</p></li><li><p><strong>真实自洽的长期游戏</strong>：如果一个游戏符合你的内在价值观，并且这个价值是你愿意一生坚守的，那么它就是一个长期游戏。查理·芒格在《穷查理宝典》中提供了一个找到这类长期游戏的切入点：在西塞罗看来，一个老人如果只愿意改进那些自己有生之年能够享受到的东西，是不体面的。对他来说，唯一值得过的人生，必须在相当程度上献给那些自己不可能亲眼看到的好结果。找到一个“自己不可能亲眼看到的好结果”，首先它强制排除了功利的选项，其次对于“好结果”的定义也需要首先理解自己的内在价值。</p></li></ol><p>这个分类指出了两种寻找长期游戏的路径：要么通过理智地分析和认识世界，要么通过仔细辨析并考验自己的内在价值。</p><p></p><p>My understanding of a long-term game is, first of all, a game that one is willing to play for a sufficiently long time. Ideally, it is a game one is willing to play for an entire life. Among the infinitely many games one can choose, a true long-term game should satisfy certain conditions. What are these conditions?</p><p>I think the kind of long-term game discussed here must at least be oriented toward something good, constructive, and beneficial. Otherwise, drug addiction and gambling would also count as obvious long-term games. More specifically, a long-term game should have the following benefits:</p><p>1. **Compounding effects**: A long-term game allows you to benefit from compound interest over time. Better skills help you develop even better skills; deeper insights allow you to see even deeper. Your investment in this game, including time, people, skills, knowledge, and wisdom, becomes increasingly efficient.</p><p>2. **Uniqueness**: A long-term game allows you to develop specific knowledge around a topic, making you irreplaceable. If this uniqueness fits market demand, the market may reward you greatly. Furthermore, uniqueness helps create accountability, which can further lead to ownership of businesses, assets, opportunities, and so on.</p><p>3. **Existential meaning**: A long-term game gives you a sense of meaning in life. It guides you through life’s ups and downs, and occupies the spare time and spare mental space that might otherwise breed emptiness and nihilism.</p><p>The method for finding a long-term game still seems unclear. At present, all I know is something like “find your interest.” We try to find ambition through interest. But interest is hard to pin down, and it changes. Often there is a “professed love without real commitment” effect: as something becomes deeper, what once seemed interesting becomes obscure and difficult, making one want to retreat. If you are lucky enough, perhaps you will encounter a field that interests you for your whole life. But this is unreliable. It seems more like bumping into something by chance, almost entirely dependent on luck.</p><p>Intuitively, a long-term game should not be purely utilitarian. Life inevitably has ups and downs, and a game you are willing to keep cultivating during low points cannot be merely utilitarian. Being utilitarian means that the goal of the game is to bring me money and keep me constantly rising. Fundamentally, this fantasy of constant upward movement conflicts with the reality of life’s fluctuations.</p><p>Besides, is it not good if this game helps me make money and escape a low point? On the one hand, not all low points are about money. Thinking that low points necessarily mean having no money may be a fantasy of those of us who are poor. On the other hand, we should also accept failure within a long-term game. The game itself should have some meaning and provide some support for life. If what I expect is for the success of the game to save me, rather than for the game itself to sustain me, then I am still trapped in a status game. This directly violates the definition of a long-term game: something I am willing to play for a lifetime. If the game cannot save me once it fails, then what I have really been playing all along is a “success game.” And the so-called game I chose may be abandoned as soon as it fails to satisfy the demands of the “success game.” Therefore, it is not truly my long-term game.</p><p>Another intuitively plausible condition is that a long-term game should align with one’s values. For people who are very clear about their values, this is obvious, and they can more easily find their long-term game. However, there are of course many people whose values are not clear. They are also confused about what they should stand for, wavering in a changing and turbulent world. For such people, relying on “values” to find a long-term game is also unreliable.</p><p>But I wonder whether values are the only path to finding a long-term game. In other words, must one first clarify one’s values before one can find a long-term game?</p><p>I think long-term games can be divided into two types:</p><p>1. **Intellectually correct long-term games**: If a game, when analyzed rationally, conforms to how the world works, aligns with first principles, and has a structure that allows it to persist as the world continues to operate, then it can become a long-term game. Of course, eternal truths do not exist, so I think it only needs to be correct over a relatively long time horizon, for example one that extends beyond an individual lifetime. Buffett’s investment philosophy is one example: it is a way of playing that structurally puts time on your side, by understanding and owning good businesses over the long term, allowing capital and judgment to compound over decades. A counterexample is high-leverage speculation, such as Jesse Livermore. It easily keeps a person exposed to the risk of ruin, and long-term participation continuously tests one’s points of fragility. Even if one is extremely intelligent, with judgment, discipline, market intuition, and experience, one may still be unable to participate indefinitely. Knowing the rules of the speculation game and being able to make enormous wealth from it does not mean that the game is suitable as a long-term game.</p><p>2. **Authentic long-term games**: If a game aligns with your inner values, and if that value is something you are willing to uphold for your entire life, then it is a long-term game. Charlie Munger, in *Poor Charlie’s Almanack*, offers a way into this kind of long-term game through Cicero: to Cicero, it was unworthy for an old man to work only to improve what he himself would live to enjoy. For him, the only life worth living was one dedicated in substantial part to good outcomes one could not possibly survive to see. Finding a “good outcome one cannot possibly survive to see” first forcibly excludes purely utilitarian options. Second, defining what counts as a “good outcome” also requires understanding one’s inner values.</p><p>This classification points to two paths for finding a long-term game: either by rationally analyzing and understanding the world, or by carefully discerning and testing one’s inner values.</p>
