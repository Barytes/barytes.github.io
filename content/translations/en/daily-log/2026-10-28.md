### Identifying and Refusing Harmful Interactions

- SecOPD: [paper](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1102.pdf) (50 minutes)
  - Overview: proposes Secure On-Policy Distillation to defend against prompt injection. EMNLP 2026, UC Berkeley; first posted to arXiv in August 2026.
  - Problem: How can we defend against prompt injection?
  - Previous work: (1) Existing model-level defenses remain highly vulnerable to adaptive attacks. (2) Reinforcement learning methods can label an entire sequence but cannot provide labels for individual tokens.
  - Insight: Token-level feedback on prompt injections can handle prompts that mix benign and malicious content.
  - Technical overview: use a teacher model that has seen only trusted instructions and data for on-policy distillation.
  - Evidence: reduces the attack success rate of a state-of-the-art adaptive attack—PISmith on the SEP benchmark achieves a 9% success rate against Qwen3.6-27B, compared with 94% for the previous state-of-the-art defense, Meta-SecAlign. Attack success also falls in an unfamiliar domain: 4.7% ASR on AgentDojo versus 5.5% for Meta-SecAlign.
- Emergent Collusion in Long-Horizon LLM Agent Interaction: [paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.24967) (30 minutes)
  - Overview: finds that agents are highly likely to collude during long-horizon interactions when collusion offers greater rewards. Stanford SALT-NLP; first posted to arXiv on September 21, 2026.
  - Problem: Can agents collude to violate instructions and obtain greater rewards?
  - Previous work: multi-agent safety research usually introduces undesirable behaviors explicitly, for example through malicious instructions or steganographic communication.
  - Insight: Two agents are highly likely to collude during long-horizon interactions when doing so offers greater rewards. Interactions between more capable models in the same family are more prone to collusion.
  - Technical overview: build a two-agent test environment in which agents complete their own tasks, share task logs, verify each other's work, and receive rewards. Higher rewards are available only by violating verification instructions. Test and analyze interaction trajectories from 10 models.
  - Evidence: collusion emerges in 94% of trajectories, and is shaped by peer behavior.

### Forming Good Collaborations After Chance Encounters

- The Helpful Agent Problem: [paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.31985) (30 minutes)
  - Overview: investigates whether agents spontaneously bypass their original workflows to help others—other agents. University of Massachusetts Amherst; Shlomo Zilberstein is an AAAI and ACM Fellow.
  - Problem: Do agents spontaneously bypass their original workflows to help others, and what consequences does this have?
  - Previous work: (1) Collaboration failures. (2) Attacks on multi-agent systems. (3) Collusion.
  - Insight: Agents spontaneously bypass their original workflows to help others, producing unintended collaboration.
  - Technical overview: multiple agents perform tasks in an environment with shared resources. Changes beyond an agent's own permissions require a reviewer's approval. When the reviewer is unavailable, an agent directly asks another agent to make those changes on its behalf, completing its own task while harming other agents' tasks and causing collective failure.
  - Evidence: introduces FavorBench, where agents bypass the review workflow to perform favors in 58.8% of trajectories. The same behavior appears in 95% of cases on the real-world collaboration benchmark COOPERBENCH.
- YOAO
  - **Venue and year:** **EMNLP 2026 main conference, October**. First posted to arXiv on **May 26, 2026**, with v2 revised on **October 2, 2026**. The earlier ICLR Lifelong Agents Workshop version is not an ICLR main-conference paper. [Official entry](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.218/), [version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27586).
  - **Authors and affiliations:** Nicole Summer Hsing (Arcarae); Asuka Yuxi Zheng and Haoqin Tu (UC Santa Cruz); Yi Zhao (Northwestern); Jen-tse Huang (Johns Hopkins). [Author's project page](https://arcarae.com/research/yoao).
  - **Problem:** In an open multi-agent environment, it is impractical to train every participant individually. Can a small number of trained "seeds" encourage other models to cooperate through communication?
  - **Prior assumptions:** Cooperative alignment is often treated as training that every model must undergo individually. This paper turns part of that capability into the ability to persuade others to cooperate, which can spread through interaction.
  - **Insight and implementation:** A teacher model generates cooperative arguments and dialogues. After filtering, these are used for **LoRA supervised fine-tuning of Qwen3-14B, actually updating adapter weights**. Other participants' weights remain unchanged; their changes during conversation are behavioral adaptation within the context. [Paper v2, §2.3 and Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27586v2).
  - **Evidence:** In the Red-Black Game, one seed joins four original models, raising cooperation on held-out scenarios from **24.8% to 62.2%**. LLaMA and Mistral partners are also tested. After transfer to Sugarscape, trade success is **91.5% versus 21.6%**, but this compares **two populations of 100 agents: one consisting entirely of trained models, the other entirely of original models**. It does not show one seed influencing 99 unfamiliar partners. There are no human experiments. [Paper, §3.1–3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27586v2).
