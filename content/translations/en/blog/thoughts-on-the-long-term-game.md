---
title: "Thoughts on the Long-Term Game"
description: "What makes a game worth playing for a lifetime? Thoughts on compounding, uniqueness, meaning, and two paths to finding a long-term game."
---

<p>My understanding of a long-term game is, first of all, a game that one is willing to play for a sufficiently long time. Ideally, it is a game one is willing to play for an entire life. Among the infinitely many games one can choose, a true long-term game should satisfy certain conditions. What are these conditions?</p><p>I think the kind of long-term game discussed here must at least be oriented toward something good, constructive, and beneficial. Otherwise, drug addiction and gambling would also count as obvious long-term games. More specifically, a long-term game should have the following benefits:</p><p>1. <strong>Compounding effects</strong>: A long-term game allows you to benefit from compound interest over time. Better skills help you develop even better skills; deeper insights allow you to see even deeper. Your investment in this game, including time, people, skills, knowledge, and wisdom, becomes increasingly efficient.</p><p>2. <strong>Uniqueness</strong>: A long-term game allows you to develop specific knowledge around a topic, making you irreplaceable. If this uniqueness fits market demand, the market may reward you greatly. Furthermore, uniqueness helps create accountability, which can further lead to ownership of businesses, assets, opportunities, and so on.</p><p>3. <strong>Existential meaning</strong>: A long-term game gives you a sense of meaning in life. It guides you through life’s ups and downs, and occupies the spare time and spare mental space that might otherwise breed emptiness and nihilism.</p><p>The method for finding a long-term game still seems unclear. At present, all I know is something like “find your interest.” We try to find ambition through interest. But interest is hard to pin down, and it changes. Often there is a “professed love without real commitment” effect: as something becomes deeper, what once seemed interesting becomes obscure and difficult, making one want to retreat. If you are lucky enough, perhaps you will encounter a field that interests you for your whole life. But this is unreliable. It seems more like bumping into something by chance, almost entirely dependent on luck.</p><p>Intuitively, a long-term game should not be purely utilitarian. Life inevitably has ups and downs, and a game you are willing to keep cultivating during low points cannot be merely utilitarian. Being utilitarian means that the goal of the game is to bring me money and keep me constantly rising. Fundamentally, this fantasy of constant upward movement conflicts with the reality of life’s fluctuations.</p><p>Besides, is it not good if this game helps me make money and escape a low point? On the one hand, not all low points are about money. Thinking that low points necessarily mean having no money may be a fantasy of those of us who are poor. On the other hand, we should also accept failure within a long-term game. The game itself should have some meaning and provide some support for life. If what I expect is for the success of the game to save me, rather than for the game itself to sustain me, then I am still trapped in a status game. This directly violates the definition of a long-term game: something I am willing to play for a lifetime. If the game cannot save me once it fails, then what I have really been playing all along is a “success game.” And the so-called game I chose may be abandoned as soon as it fails to satisfy the demands of the “success game.” Therefore, it is not truly my long-term game.</p><p>Another intuitively plausible condition is that a long-term game should align with one’s values. For people who are very clear about their values, this is obvious, and they can more easily find their long-term game. However, there are of course many people whose values are not clear. They are also confused about what they should stand for, wavering in a changing and turbulent world. For such people, relying on “values” to find a long-term game is also unreliable.</p><p>But I wonder whether values are the only path to finding a long-term game. In other words, must one first clarify one’s values before one can find a long-term game?</p><p>I think long-term games can be divided into two types:</p><p>1. <strong>Intellectually correct long-term games</strong>: If a game, when analyzed rationally, conforms to how the world works, aligns with first principles, and has a structure that allows it to persist as the world continues to operate, then it can become a long-term game. Of course, eternal truths do not exist, so I think it only needs to be correct over a relatively long time horizon, for example one that extends beyond an individual lifetime. Buffett’s investment philosophy is one example: it is a way of playing that structurally puts time on your side, by understanding and owning good businesses over the long term, allowing capital and judgment to compound over decades. A counterexample is high-leverage speculation, such as Jesse Livermore. It easily keeps a person exposed to the risk of ruin, and long-term participation continuously tests one’s points of fragility. Even if one is extremely intelligent, with judgment, discipline, market intuition, and experience, one may still be unable to participate indefinitely. Knowing the rules of the speculation game and being able to make enormous wealth from it does not mean that the game is suitable as a long-term game.</p><p>2. <strong>Authentic long-term games</strong>: If a game aligns with your inner values, and if that value is something you are willing to uphold for your entire life, then it is a long-term game. Charlie Munger, in <em>Poor Charlie’s Almanack</em>, offers a way into this kind of long-term game through Cicero: to Cicero, it was unworthy for an old man to work only to improve what he himself would live to enjoy. For him, the only life worth living was one dedicated in substantial part to good outcomes one could not possibly survive to see. Finding a “good outcome one cannot possibly survive to see” first forcibly excludes purely utilitarian options. Second, defining what counts as a “good outcome” also requires understanding one’s inner values.</p><p>This classification points to two paths for finding a long-term game: either by rationally analyzing and understanding the world, or by carefully discerning and testing one’s inner values.</p>
