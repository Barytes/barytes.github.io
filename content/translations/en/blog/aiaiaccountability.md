## TL;DR

AI can amplify an individual’s capability and judgment hundreds or thousands of times. Yet in an unequal working relationship, it can also become a superior’s whip for piling endless work onto a subordinate. The difference lies in how much accountability a person has for their work. If they have the authority to decide, AI amplifies their capability. If they cannot decide what their work should be, AI becomes the whip their superior uses on them.

## Background

I have recently been revising a paper that has tormented me for a long time. The experiments were already fully written up, and I was doing everything I could to finish and submit it. After reading it, however, my supervisor unexpectedly commented that two sets of experiments both compared changes in user numbers, and that one should use a different parameter as its independent variable.

I felt overwhelmed. On the one hand, I did not want to rewrite any part of it again. On the other, I thought varying user numbers in both experiments was reasonable; other parameters would not support our key conclusions as well.

When I said I did not want to keep doing this, my supervisor responded: “You can use ChatGPT.” As someone who already cannot do without AI, I immediately felt a kind of dark humor, as if my supervisor had just whipped me with AI.

On the surface, this was a suggestion about a tool. In practice, it changed the nature of the discussion. We should have been discussing whether the experiment was worth doing and what success would mean. Instead, the question became: “Since you have AI, why can’t you finish faster?”

I call this the AI whip phenomenon.

## What is the AI whip?

The AI whip is what happens when AI enters an asymmetric working relationship and becomes a tool for superiors to add tasks, reduce their perception of cost, and weaken subordinates’ authority to exercise judgment.

AI does not issue orders directly here. It changes how people imagine the cost of labor. Because AI appears able to complete many kinds of work quickly, a superior more readily assumes that additional work has become cheap. Low-value additions, vague requirements, and frequent changes gain a new justification: “Just have AI do it.”

To explain this phenomenon, the key is to examine two conditions rather than just ask whether AI can complete a task:

1. Does the person doing the work have full accountability?
2. Does the person making the request understand the real costs and organizational consequences of using AI?

These conditions determine whether AI amplifies the worker’s judgment or the superior’s demands.

## Condition 1: having accountability

I am borrowing the concept of accountability from *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*. Here, accountability means whether a person has decision-making authority over their work. It has five parts:

- **Authority to define the goal:** being able to explain what problem the work should solve.
- **Authority to judge value:** deciding whether something is worth doing, rather than only whether it can be done.
- **Authority to choose the approach:** deciding which methods, tools, and pace to use.
- **Authority to stop:** being able to say “this is enough” when marginal benefits are insufficient.
- **Authority to explain results:** explaining the final outcome, reasons for failure, and tradeoffs.

Someone who bears delivery responsibility without these powers is an executor rather than an owner. They carry the pressure of results while being unable to decide the work itself. AI sharpens this structure: it makes superiors more likely to think “technology has reduced execution costs,” further weakening a subordinate’s voice on value, boundaries, and stopping conditions.

Accountability therefore determines whom AI amplifies.

If a team member has accountability, AI amplifies that person’s own judgment. They can use it for search, generation, experimentation, automation, and verification, while deciding for themselves what is worth doing, how far to take it, and how to explain the results.

If the member lacks accountability, AI amplifies the superior’s demands. The superior can add tasks at lower cost to themselves, while the subordinate is expected to use AI to absorb the additions. AI becomes a means of weakening the member’s judgment rather than an assistant that helps them act on it.

## Condition 2: a superior’s understanding of AI

Understanding AI means more than knowing it can write code, edit text, or generate experiment scripts. It means recognizing that AI rearranges costs, responsibilities, and verification within a workflow. Specifically, **using AI creates new intermediate costs**. Many people see its power without seeing the costs of clarifying requirements, verifying results, and fixing mistakes. Superiors often underestimate these costs, while the people carrying out the work bear them. AI may seem to reduce costs to a manager while creating unfamiliar new costs for the executor—costs that are not necessarily easier to handle than those of a traditional workflow.

### How a superior uses AI becomes how they treat subordinates

A deeper layer of the AI whip is that a superior’s way of using AI carries over into their way of working with people.

Someone who uses AI with clear standards will first define goals, constraints, acceptance criteria, and stopping conditions, then ask AI to execute. In management, they are more likely to treat subordinates as collaborators capable of judgment: explaining why the work matters, how far it should go, what outcome has value, and which directions need no further effort.

Someone who uses AI as a wish-granting machine becomes accustomed to changing requirements, adding requests, and asking for repeated generations until something looks satisfactory. In management, a subordinate becomes a new AI agent: someone who can be repeatedly prompted, expected to fill in their own context, and asked to keep trying under vague goals.

The subordinate may even become a “tool for directing more AI agents.” The superior does not directly face AI’s failures, rework, and verification costs. Instead, these costs pass to the subordinate: ask ChatGPT, revise the prompt, run the experiment, judge whether the result is usable. The person is compressed into an intermediary scheduler, translating vague wishes into tasks AI can execute and turning unstable AI outputs into deliverable results.

The central issue is whether a superior extends the mindset of “I can prompt AI however I like” into “I can prompt people however I like.”

## How the two conditions combine into a whip

Accountability and understanding AI are not isolated factors. They amplify each other within a working relationship.

Without accountability, the worker has difficulty stopping a task on the grounds that it lacks value. If the requester also misunderstands AI’s real costs, they use it as a reason to assume costs are low. Together, these produce the AI whip:

1. The superior retains the authority to add requirements.
2. The executor bears the costs of implementation, verification, and explanation.
3. AI reduces the superior’s perception of cost.
4. AI does not automatically reduce the executor’s pressure to answer for results.
5. Work whose value should have been discussed becomes “Why aren’t you using AI to finish faster?”

AI then makes the asymmetric working relationship run more smoothly, rather than improving the quality of collaboration.

## AI-native and non-AI-native teams through accountability

From this perspective, I think the fundamental difference between AI-native and non-AI-native teams is whether the organization gives accountability to the people actually doing the work.

A team may have everyone using ChatGPT and still not be AI-native. If a few superiors can define goals arbitrarily, requirements can drift endlessly, and executors have no authority to stop or judge value, AI has merely been connected to the old organizational structure. Better collaboration will not follow naturally; the old structure will simply run faster.

A truly AI-native team should treat AI as an amplifier of execution and exploration. It must therefore also delegate authority to exercise judgment. Otherwise, stronger execution only draws members deeper into a stream of endless additions.

### Non-AI-native teams

Common features include:

- Superiors keep authority over goals; subordinates bear execution responsibility.
- Requirements remain verbal, vague, and frequently changing.
- Members may use AI but cannot decide what is not worth doing.
- The organization rewards faster delivery rather than clarifying problems or refusing low-value work.
- Executors receive pressure to deliver without authority to stop.
- The AI process is invisible, and individuals absorb rework and verification costs.

In such teams, the AI whip appears in forms such as:

- “AI makes this quick, right?” becomes a reason to add work.
- The requester leaves standards unclear and expects the executor to fill them in with AI.
- Difficulties are interpreted as an inability to use AI or resistance to new tools.
- Individuals absorb rework instead of returning to clarify requirements.
- AI’s apparent capability legitimizes vague management.

These teams have attached a faster execution interface to the lack of clarity, asymmetry, and arbitrary escalation in traditional management.

People become a kind of “human-shaped agent.” The superior supplies a vague prompt; the subordinate fills in context, schedules tools, calls AI, filters results, fixes errors, and bears responsibility. On the surface, the person has gained AI tools. In practice, the organization treats them as a more advanced AI scheduler.

### AI-native teams

AI-native teams are closer to an accountability-first organization. Alongside AI access, they give the people doing the work judgment authority that matches AI’s capabilities:

- Members can question the goal rather than merely receive tasks.
- Members can define acceptance criteria rather than only await evaluation.
- Members can choose tools and approaches rather than only perform prescribed actions.
- Members can reject low-value additions rather than endlessly absorb requirement drift.
- Members can explain failures and tradeoffs rather than only answer for unfinished work.

In these teams, AI is more like an assistant than a whip. The reason is clearer ownership of accountability, rather than gentler management. AI amplifies the member’s ownership instead of the superior’s passing ideas.
