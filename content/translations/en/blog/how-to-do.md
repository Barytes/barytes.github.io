---
title: "How to Do"
description: "Most things we want to do stay stuck as slogans. Use “build something” and “talk to somebody” to test whether the level of abstraction is too high or too low, and find a problem you can actually act on."
---

If you want to do something, how exactly do you do it?

Often, the things we want to do are vague and abstract in our heads—even if they sound wonderful and correct, like Paul Graham’s advice to “make good new things.” When you actually try to get your hands dirty, you usually have to rely on a highly heuristic approach, just doing whatever pops into your head. Meanwhile, most advice online stops at equally abstract slogans like “just take action,” which utterly fails to tell you what “action” actually is. It’s like how being told to “eat healthy” doesn’t tell you what exactly you should eat. So, what should you actually do?

My thought is this: first, find a problem you can control. It must sit at the right **level of abstraction**. Trying to directly “make good new things,” “master AI,” or “get a PhD” are examples of the level of abstraction being too high, making them impossible to act upon. Conversely, if the level of abstraction is too low, it also leads to inaction—for instance, “start by eating a good meal” or “just write two lines of code” (which happens to be the kind of life advice LLMs love to give). You will feel that doing these things is completely useless for solving your actual problem.

So, what is the right level of abstraction? I don’t think you can define it perfectly right off the bat, but you can test whether your current level of abstraction is too high or too low. This test relies on two anchors: **build something**, and **talk to somebody**.

If your level of abstraction is too high, you simply won’t be able to explicitly answer these two questions: 1) What is the thing you can build? 2) Who is the real person you can talk to?

Being able to answer **“what is the thing you can build”** means you completely understand the underlying mechanisms, can explain the whole thing to others, can answer their questions, and can manually debug and fix it yourself. You don’t necessarily have to be able to say all this *before* you build it, but you absolutely must be able to say it *after*. The advent of AI has inflated people’s ambitions, making them think they can build much larger things. But the result often exceeds what they can actually grasp, leading them to either give up entirely or keep pretending to work while drowning in technical debt.

And **“a real person you can talk to”** means exactly what it says: you must be able to name them, have their contact info, and actually be able to reach out and converse with them. There are two interesting cases here:

1. *Famous people:* You might know their name and contact info, but you can’t guarantee they’ll reply. However, if you do manage to reach them and have a conversation, they are no longer an abstract entity. In a way, this becomes an achievement in itself (“Hey, I just chatted with Elon Musk for two hours”).
2. *Abstract groups:* For example, “users with X pain point” or “people doing Y.” This is actually just a hint, sitting at too high a level of abstraction. You need to follow this hint to find a specific, real person in order to lower it to an actionable level.

If your level of abstraction is too low, how do you test that? Try imagining a third party in your head and explaining what you are doing to them: tell this third party about the thing you are building and the person you are talking to, and see if they would think your actions are actually helping your original goal. You don’t even need to find a real person to do this (though doing so has its benefits)—if, in your imagination, the moment you open your mouth you yourself feel that what you are doing is completely unhelpful and a pure waste of time and energy, then it truly is unhelpful. This means your level of abstraction is too low.

I believe that things at too high a level of abstraction can be completely ignored in real life—topics like the AI doomsday, AI replacing jobs, or how to find true love. On the other hand, things at too low a level of abstraction might be highly meaningful from the perspective of mindfulness, spirituality, or everyday health—like mindfully eating every meal or getting a good workout. But if you have a specific theme you care deeply about, I imagine you are eager to do things that directly push that theme forward, and tasks at too low an abstraction level simply won’t provide that direct thrust. For the things you actually want to get done, you simply need to convert them to the right level of abstraction and find the right question.
