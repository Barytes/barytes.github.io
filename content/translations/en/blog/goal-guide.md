---
title: "A Brief Guide to Setting Goals"
description: "Set goals through autonomy, competence, and feedback, using daily records to inform your next actions."
---

Setting goals helps us feel more certain that we can shape and change our lives, do things we find enjoyable and meaningful, and build confidence in our abilities by meeting challenges [1,2]:

- Autonomy: Help yourself do more of what you like, what you choose, what you want to happen, or what will not happen unless you consciously take the initiative.
- Competence: An anchor for your attention, directing you toward actions just slightly beyond your current abilities.
- Feedback loop: Record and evaluate your ability, approach, and results, then adjust your next plan to better support autonomy and competence.

How should you set goals?

- Use them alongside daily records. Record your everyday actions—for example, what you do each hour—and score the metrics you care about, such as mood and efficiency.
- Write down only actions you strongly want to happen, or actions that definitely will not happen unless you write them down.
- You do not need to write down: 1) things already on your calendar, such as meetings; 2) habitual actions, such as brushing your teeth and washing your face; 3) a plan for every moment. Fewer items mean more focus.
- Reduce friction in decision-making: 1) decide in advance how to respond to situations you may encounter, and write these decisions as if–then statements; 2) turn the target action into an easier prerequisite—for example, start studying → go to a café 15 minutes beforehand.
- Review afterward: Did your recorded actions exceed or fall short of the goal? Adjust the goal and raise or lower its difficulty. Treat obstacles that prevented completion as problems to solve. The ultimate purpose is to maintain your sense of competence, rather than punish yourself into meeting every goal.

References

[1] Self-determination theory proposes three psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

[2] Behavioural activation theory proposes that people with depression engage in fewer activities, giving themselves fewer opportunities to experience pleasure (autonomy), competence, and connection (relatedness), which deepens their depression. Behavioural activation is a way out of this negative feedback loop: first, use records to recognize the relationship between everyday behaviour and mood; then, understand your values to identify things you want and enjoy doing; finally, increase activity by doing more of what you enjoy, creating more opportunities to experience autonomy, competence, and connection.
