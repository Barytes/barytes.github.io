---
title: "How to Work 100 Hours a Week"
description: "What a 100-hour workweek means for sleep, everyday life, and the way you work."
---

Elon Musk says to work like hell: people working over 100 hours a week can accomplish twice as much as those working 50 hours, greatly improving their chances of success [[1]](#ref-1). How do you work 100 hours a week?

Working 100 hours a week means giving up a two-day weekend and working at least 14 hours every day. If you work five days a week, you need to work 100/5 = 20 hours a day. That is clearly unreasonable. If you work six days, you need 100/6 = 16.6667 hours a day. Working seven days gives you 100/7 = 14 hours a day. Assuming the longest tolerable workday is 18 hours, working 100 hours a week means working six or seven days, with each day ranging roughly from 14 to 18 hours.

Working 14–18 hours a day leaves only 6–10 hours. You need to handle physical needs such as eating, showering, and sleeping, as well as leisure, family, commuting, exercise, and other matters. You would need to be a short sleeper: three hours of sleep leaves 3–7 hours, while six hours leaves 0–4 hours. For a typical adult, 3–6 hours of sleep is seriously insufficient, especially when that is how much you sleep every day. In 2015, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the Sleep Research Society (SRS) recommended at least seven hours of sleep each night for adults aged 18–64 [[2]](#ref-2). Insufficient sleep causes declining cognitive performance [[3]](#ref-3), irritability [[4]](#ref-4), changes in risk preferences [[5]](#ref-5), and poorer inhibitory control [[6]](#ref-6). It is also associated with weight gain and obesity, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease and stroke, depression, and increased all-cause mortality [[2]](#ref-2).

If you sleep 6–7 hours a day, only 0–3 hours remain. Allow one hour for eating, showering, and other physical needs, and you have at most two hours of free time for everything else. This means:

- You cannot cook for yourself. Preparing ingredients, cooking, and washing up takes about 1–2 hours for one meal, excluding eating. You will probably rely on someone cooking for you, a canteen, or delivery.
- You cannot look after cats or dogs, do housework, or tidy your room. These small chores can easily take more than an hour.
- You cannot have additional hobbies unless you treat them as work. Spending two hours gaming, for example, is extremely easy—and may still feel insufficient.
- Your communication with your family or partner is very limited. Two hours of companionship a day may sound like a lot, but imagine telling your partner, “I only give you two hours a day, while I spend seven to nine times that working.” How would they react?
- You probably have little time for exercise, or can only exercise lightly. Insufficient sleep reduces exercise performance [[7]](#ref-7); exercise causes fatigue, which reduces work efficiency and lengthens sleep.
- You cannot commute, or your commute must be measured in minutes. A two-hour round trip sounds perfectly plausible—but then you have no time for anything else. This means you can only freelance or sleep on the factory floor.
- You cannot indulge in modern bad habits such as scrolling through short videos, because you cannot afford to spend your precious time on them.
- You also have no spare time for leisure or recovering from burnout, such as taking a walk or wandering around.

Even working just 12 hours a day leaves only 4–5 hours after sleeping, eating, and showering. This seems a little more reasonable. It may also be the limit an ordinary person can tolerate: after commuting and chores, there is still a little personal time to recover. This may explain why workplaces have converged on 996 rather than trying to extend it to something like 7117.

What would those 14–18 hours of work look like? First, you must enjoy your work or find it deeply meaningful and necessary. Otherwise, it is hard to imagine anyone willingly overworking to this extent for long. Your way of working must be sustainable, including the following:

- You must have some autonomy over the pace and content of your work. For example, you can schedule uninterrupted deep work or initiate a team meeting. If you cannot control the pace or content, you will find yourself in an unpredictable situation full of repeated interruptions, increasing stress responses and pressure [[8]](#ref-8)[[9]](#ref-9).
- Your schedule includes some time for deep concentration, allowing you to work for extended periods in a state of flow. If your attention is repeatedly interrupted and you keep switching contexts, your brain will become exhausted and unable to work that long [[10]](#ref-10).
- Your work must include some moments of slacking off. You cannot sustain concentrated mental labour continuously for that long. The psychomotor vigilance task (PVT) has shown that after about 17 hours awake, sustained attention is roughly comparable to that at a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05% [[11]](#ref-11). In other words, during the final two or three hours of an 18-hour day, your state is close to driving under the influence. More troublingly, after prolonged wakefulness, the brain develops local sleep—slow waves in localized cortical areas [[12]](#ref-12). Attention lapses involuntarily [[13]](#ref-13), even while your eyes remain open, and you may even be unable to stay awake at all. Those 14–18 hours therefore must include intermittent breaks or changes of task.

Most of us probably cannot sustain a 100-hour workweek. But I think everyone can benefit from trying to understand what a life voluntarily devoted to something actually looks like. For the vast majority of Chinese people, experiences of concentrating intensely on one thing have been imposed on them: the university entrance exam, postgraduate entrance exams, job hunting, or exams for civil service and public institution jobs. If a little reflection reveals that wholehearted commitment—especially to something you have chosen—is less elusive and frightening than you imagined, isn't that a good thing? Or if you discover that it really is frightening and you do not want that life, isn't choosing an ordinary job and a life of your own also a good thing?
