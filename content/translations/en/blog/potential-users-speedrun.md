---
title: "A 1h Speedrun to Find Potential Users: Two Outreach Goals I Confused"
description: "An hour spent finding potential users for interview-prep-like-a-builder: public searches did not yield the trial participants I expected, but helped me separate finding peers from attracting users."
---

I did a 1h speedrun to find potential users. My goal was to **reach potential users for the interview-prep-like-a-builder skill. I mean “reach” broadly: get people's attention and make it visible to people who might need it.** At the same time, I wanted to get to know more experienced practitioners and peers already thinking about and working on the same problem.

After an hour, the route I had imagined for reaching potential users had not worked out, but the search for practitioners and peers had been useful. More significantly, I realized I had mixed these two goals together and, without comparing alternatives, defaulted to the outreach method I knew best.

## 1. The initial hypothesis: find a publicly expressed pain point, solve it, then demonstrate value.

The route I initially imagined was:

> Find someone who recently posted about difficulties with job hunting, their résumé, or projects → test the skill against their specific problem → produce a result to show them → establish a connection.

I wanted to find real people I could contact, rather than start with an imagined user persona. So I began with Superlinear Academy and X, looking for three potential users and two people doing similar work. Later, I narrowed the search to people who felt the projects on their résumés were too much like “toy projects,” hoping to make the problem more specific.

Several assumptions in this approach went unchecked:

- Potential users would describe their difficulties publicly and in enough detail.
- Public posts would provide enough background to start helping.
- Producing something tailored to a person would let them experience the skill's value.
- Similar search and outreach methods would work for both potential users and experienced practitioners or peers.

I did not treat these as hypotheses to test. I went straight into execution: searching posts, screening candidates, and finding original posts and ways to contact people.

## 2. What I found: plenty of relevant discussion, few accounts of the kind we needed.

After reading the posts we found and the discussions on Superlinear about preparing résumés and finding work in the AI era, my impression was that people generally agreed on grounding projects and résumés in real problems and emphasized making something substantial. Few, however, described exactly how they were preparing, where they were stuck, or why.

On Superlinear, most of what I saw was people sharing projects. Those who had not built a project, or did not intend to share one, might leave a single comment and have little information on their profiles. Their actual difficulties and concerns might never be stated openly.

The people and interactions on X were interesting, but more of the posts we saw were direct requests for project problems or ideas. Some replies described people's own problems; others were casual interactions and jokes. These posts could provide leads, but they did not mean someone was ready to discuss their background in detail and commit to an ongoing process of using the skill together.

Of the three candidates we eventually found, only one explicitly wanted to move beyond tutorial projects. The other two were asking for project directions that would add value to their résumés. These were relevant leads, but they were still some distance from the people I had hoped to find: people who had described their project difficulties concretely enough for us to begin a test directly.

What this finding shows is that **the public search route we chose did not readily provide the information this experiment needed.** It is not enough to conclude that these people have no need for the product.

## 3. Another problem emerged: producing something for someone may not demonstrate the value I want to test.

This skill needs a person to use it, participate, and iterate. It aims to give someone who does not know where to start some scaffolding, help them engage sooner with a real problem they care about, and gradually develop the ability to understand, judge, and deliver.

If I automatically generated a plan or project based on a single post, I might simply add another piece of AI content that nobody cares about. The recipient might not be interested, and they would not have gone through the process the skill is actually meant to help them complete.

This changed what I wanted from demonstration materials: I needed to show **how a person uses the skill to move from being stuck to making progress on a problem.**

But that does not directly imply that “this kind of product suits public content and does not suit direct outreach.” Two things need to be separated here: how a product creates value affects how to try it and demonstrate it; whether an outreach method fits also depends on users' psychology and habits, and the relationship I hope to establish.

## 4. What I really confused: two outreach goals and one default route.

I initially had user acquisition in its broad sense in mind, but only one method occurred to me:

> Find a need the other person has explicitly expressed, then offer value tailored to it.

This resembles the approach I know from reaching out to prospective academic supervisors or sending cold emails: first identify someone accomplished and relevant, study what they are doing, then explain what I can do and what we might discuss.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that route. Showing work publicly is another valid route: I can write about what I am doing in a blog or post, let more people see it, and form connections from there.

Take McDonald's fries: you can treat someone to a portion, or film someone enjoying them. **The same product can attract attention in both ways. The product's nature cannot be the only thing that determines the approach.**

This time, I treated “find experienced practitioners and peers worth getting to know” and “get people who might need help to notice the product” as the same problem.

In the language I used at the time, the former was more of an “upward” problem: I already knew a particular person was worth getting to know and wanted to establish contact. The latter was more of a “downward” problem: I wanted to put my practice and help out into the world so an as-yet-undefined group could see it and become interested.

In this setting, my inclination is: **upward, find specific people first, then start a focused conversation; downward, show my real practice first, so the right people can recognize their own situation.** This is a judgment from this exploration. It does not need to become a rule that holds in every setting.

## 5. What worked: I did find people focused on the same problem.

The hour was not entirely misdirected. The search for experienced practitioners and peers produced useful results.

For example, Challen discusses how real projects and the work process can build credibility in interviews. Matt Dancho focuses on the gap between toy projects and real business problems and teaches in this area. They have already expressed their interests publicly, so searching by topic, reading about their practice, and finding a starting point for conversation is a viable route.

These people are worth contacting further. So far, though, I have identified relevant people; I have not yet started a conversation.

This also helped me see that **“finding relevant people” is an achievement, but it cannot directly stand in for “finding potential users willing to use the product.”** Progress toward the two goals should be evaluated separately.

## 6. The next adjustment: use myself as the example, complete a real trial, then share the case.

I want to change the approach: start with a problem I truly care about, use the skill myself, participate in solving the problem, produce something real, then share both the output and the process. I can borrow from content creators and let people understand what I am doing through a concrete case first.

The case needs to show:

- Why I care about the problem and where I was initially stuck.
- Where the skill helped.
- What judgments, attempts, and changes I made myself.
- What moved forward in the end, and what remains unresolved.

That way, the material I share is itself a record of a real trial. It first answers “How did this help me?” and then lets people in a similar situation decide whether it is worth trying. After that, I can still actively send the case to specific people or invite interested people to use the skill together.

I will also keep looking for experienced practitioners and peers, using concrete results and questions to start conversations. These two routes can coexist while serving different purposes.

## 7. Before choosing an outreach method next time, I want to make a few judgments.

**First, be clear about the relationship I want to establish this time.**\
Do I want to ask a particular person for advice or collaboration, or get an as-yet-undefined group to notice the product? Had I separated these goals first, I would not have used the same search process for both.

**Look at how the target audience actually expresses itself, discovers things, and participates.**\
Do they ask for help publicly, or prefer to share results? Do they describe their difficulties on their own, or say “I have this problem too” only after seeing someone else's case? This time, the entry point should follow observed behavior, rather than the expectation that people will express their needs in a way that fits my acquisition process.

**Check whether the next step depends on a lot of guesswork.**\
If I only have one comment but need to fill in someone's background, goals, and constraints to provide “tailored help,” the lead is not yet sufficient to support that action. Searching for more similar posts may not solve the problem.

**Evaluate the results of each goal separately.**\
Finding peers worth talking to, getting potential users' attention, finding someone willing to try the product, and having someone actually benefit from it are different kinds of progress. This time, the first part succeeded. The later parts still need new experiments.

The lesson I most want to keep from this attempt is: **when thinking about “how to get people's attention,” I too quickly applied the familiar route of “study a specific person first, then offer them value.” Next time, I need to first understand the people I want to reach and how they naturally behave, then decide whether to approach them directly or first create a case that makes them want to come closer.**
